Part I · Chapters 01–09

Foundations

Before agents there is one function: text in, one more piece of text out. Everything else in this atlas is built on top of that single trick.

01Beginner

The story so far

Seventy-six years in one scroll

AI did not arrive in 2022. It is a relay of ideas that kept stalling for lack of data and computing power, then sprinted once both showed up. Knowing the order makes every later chapter easier.

Pattern one

Ideas wait for hardware

Neural networks were invented in 1958. They only worked at scale in 2012, when gaming GPUs and internet-sized datasets arrived.

Pattern two

Scale was the surprise

From 2018 to 2023 the recipe barely changed. Models and data got bigger, and abilities appeared that nobody programmed.

Pattern three

From answers to actions

2024 to 2026 is the agent era. Models stopped only replying and started running tools, writing code and finishing multi-hour tasks.

Takeaway

The core model idea is old. What changed is scale, and then the software we wrap around models. That wrapping is what an AI engineer builds.

02Beginner

How machines learn

A function with a billion knobs

Numbers go in, numbers come out. In between are layers of simple units called neurons. Every connection has a weight, a knob that sets how much one neuron listens to another. Learning means turning the knobs until the outputs are right.

One neuron

Multiply, add, squash

output = activation(w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + w₃·x₃ + bias)

0.8·1.0 + (−0.5)·0.2 + 0.3·0.9 + 0.1 = 1.07
ReLU keeps positives → 1.07

That is all a neuron does. Intelligence comes from wiring billions of them together and tuning the weights.

Scale

How many knobs?

GPT-2, 2019: 1.5 billion parameters.
GPT-3, 2020: 175 billion.
Frontier models, 2026: sizes are no longer published; estimates run from hundreds of billions to several trillion.

A parameter is one weight. "A 70B model" means seventy billion knobs.

Term

Loss

A single number for how wrong the model is. Training exists to shrink it.

Term

Backpropagation

The calculus that works out, for every weight at once, which direction reduces the loss.

Term

Overfitting

Memorising training examples instead of learning the pattern. Looks perfect in training, fails on new data.

Takeaway

Nobody writes the rules inside a neural network. We write the goal, and gradient descent finds the weights. That is why models can surprise us.

03Beginner

Tokens & embeddings

Models read tokens, then turn them into places

Step one: chop text into tokens, common chunks of characters. Step two: turn each token into a long list of numbers called an embedding, a coordinate in meaning-space. Similar meanings land near each other.

Why it matters

Tokens are the unit of cost and memory

You pay per token, speed is tokens per second, and context windows are counted in tokens. A 200K window holds roughly 150,000 English words.

Why it matters

Embeddings power search

Embed a question and a million documents, then find the nearest. That is semantic search, the engine inside RAG (chapter 11) and many memory systems (chapter 16).

Takeaway

Text becomes tokens, tokens become vectors, and close vectors mean close meanings. Think in tokens, not words, whenever you reason about cost or limits.

04Beginner

Attention & the Transformer

Every word looks at every other word

In 2017 a Google paper, "Attention Is All You Need", introduced the Transformer. Its core move: for each token, compute how much to attend to every other token, then blend in their information. Context resolves meaning.

Why it won

Parallel, not sequential

Older networks read one word at a time, like reading through a straw. Transformers see the whole passage at once, so training runs across thousands of GPUs in parallel.

Depth

Layers stack meaning

Early layers track grammar and neighbours. Middle layers hold facts and relationships. Late layers plan the next token. Frontier models stack around a hundred.

Cost

Every pair costs

Every token attending to every other means cost climbs quickly with length. That is why context has limits and long prompts are slower and pricier.

Beyond text

Anything can be tokens

Images, audio and video are cut into tokens too, so the same architecture reads them. That is what "multimodal" means (chapter 09).

Takeaway

Attention is how a model uses context. Everything you put in the prompt can shape every token it writes, for better or worse.

05Beginner

How an LLM writes

One token at a time, rolling weighted dice

A large language model does one thing: given all the text so far, it scores every token in its vocabulary for what comes next. A sampler picks one, the token is appended, and it runs again. A paragraph is a few hundred rolls of the dice.

Consequence

Hallucination

The model picks what is plausible, not what is true. When it lacks a fact, a confident guess still scores well. Give it sources, tools and permission to say "I don't know".

Consequence

It is stateless

Nothing carries over between calls. Every call re-reads the whole conversation from the top. Chat "memory" is the app re-sending history. This one fact explains most of Part III.

Consequence

Thinking is more tokens

Reasoning models write a private scratchpad before answering. Spending more tokens thinking often gives better answers on hard problems (chapter 08).

Takeaway

An LLM is a next-token predictor inside a loop that appends. No database of facts, no memory between calls: just very well-trained guessing over the context you provide.

06Intermediate

How an LLM is made

From internet autocomplete to careful assistant

A modern model is trained in stages. Each stage takes the same network and nudges it toward a different goal. Step through them and watch one question get four very different answers.

Two phases

Training vs inference

Training happens once: months on tens of thousands of GPUs, costing hundreds of millions of dollars. Inference happens every time you send a message: seconds and fractions of a cent.

Access

Open weights vs closed

Closed models (Claude, GPT, Gemini) run behind an API. Open-weight models (Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, Mistral, gpt-oss) can be downloaded and run on your own hardware.

Adaptation

Fine-tuning, LoRA, distillation

Fine-tuning continues training on your data. LoRA does it cheaply with small add-on weights. Distillation teaches a small model to copy a big one. Quantization shrinks weights for cheaper chips.

Takeaway

Pre-training gives knowledge. Post-training gives behaviour. Most of the personality and safety you notice comes from post-training.

07Intermediate

Compute & scaling

Every word costs billions of multiplications

Under the hood a model is mostly one operation, repeated: multiply two grids of numbers and add up the results. GPUs are machines built to do exactly that, thousands of times in parallel. Scale it up and you get both the capability and the bill.

Inference, part 1

Prefill

The model reads your whole prompt in one parallel sweep. Fast per token, and it sets the time to first token (TTFT).

Inference, part 2

Decode

Then it writes one token at a time, each step reading its own past. Output tokens are slower and pricier than input tokens, typically about five times the price.

Trick

KV cache

Attention results for earlier tokens are stored, so each new token doesn't recompute the whole prompt. Prompt caching (chapter 15) reuses this across requests.

Economics

Batching

Servers run many users' requests through the GPU together. That's why APIs are cheap per token, and why batch jobs get discounts.

Takeaway

Compute is the raw material of intelligence. It explains the price of every token, the speed of every reply, and why a few companies spend tens of billions on data centres.

08Intermediate

Reasoning models

Thinking before speaking

Since late 2024, frontier models can spend extra tokens working through a problem privately before they answer: trying approaches, checking, backtracking. More thinking usually means better answers on hard problems, paid for in time and tokens.

How it's trained

Rewarded for being right

Reinforcement learning on problems with checkable answers (maths, code with tests). Chains of thought that reach verified answers are reinforced.

Turn it up for

Hard, multi-step work

Maths, debugging, planning, analysis, long agent runs. APIs expose an "effort" setting that trades depth for speed and cost.

Turn it down for

Simple or instant tasks

Lookups, classification, chat replies, anything latency-critical. Extra thinking there just adds cost.

In agents

Interleaved thinking

Agents think between tool calls: read a result, reconsider, then act. That is much of why 2025–26 agents became reliable.

Takeaway

Intelligence now has a dial. You can buy better answers with more thinking tokens, so match the effort to the difficulty of the task.

09Intermediate

Multimodal & generation

Seeing, hearing, and making images

The same ideas reach beyond text. Images, audio and video are cut into tokens so language models can read them. Image and video generators mostly work another way: they start from pure noise and remove it step by step, steered by your prompt.

Input

Vision-language models

LLMs that read screenshots, charts, PDFs and photos. This is what makes computer-use agents possible (chapter 20).

Voice

Two architectures

A pipeline (speech-to-text → LLM → text-to-speech) is flexible. Native speech-to-speech models are faster and keep tone of voice. Real-time voice agents use both.

Output

Images and video

Diffusion and flow-matching models generate images, video and 3D. Many chat assistants now call them as tools.

Search

Embeddings for everything

Images, audio and text can share one embedding space, so you can search photos with a sentence.

Takeaway

Language models read every medium by turning it into tokens. Generators create media by removing noise. Most products combine the two.

Part II · Chapters 10–12

Talking to models

The model is fixed. What you control is what goes into its context window. These chapters are about putting the right things in.

10Intermediate

Prompts & context windows

The context window is the model's entire world

For each call, the model sees one long sequence of tokens: instructions, tool descriptions, past messages, documents and your new message. Nothing else exists for it. The window is how many tokens fit, typically 200,000 to 1,000,000 in 2026.

Anatomy of a good prompt

Role & audienceYou are a support agent for Acme's billing team. Customers are small-business owners.
ContextRefund policy: full refund within 30 days; after that, account credit only. <policy>…</policy>
TaskDraft a reply to the ticket below. Decide refund or credit using the policy.
FormatReturn JSON: {"decision": "refund" | "credit", "reply": string}
Examples<example>Ticket: bought 12 days ago… → {"decision":"refund",…}</example>
ConstraintsNever promise dates. If the order ID is missing, ask for it instead of guessing.

Write for a smart new hire. The model is brilliant but knows nothing about your company, your customer or your taste. Anything you would tell a new colleague, tell the model.

Explain why. "Keep it short because customers read on phones" works better than "BE SHORT". Reasons generalise; shouting doesn't.

Show, don't just tell. Two or three examples fix format and tone faster than paragraphs of rules.

More is not better. Long, noisy contexts dilute attention. Details buried in the middle get missed, and quality drifts as windows fill ("context rot").

Takeaway

Prompt engineering is writing clear instructions. Context engineering, in chapter 15, is deciding everything else that goes into the window.

11Intermediate

Retrieval-augmented generation

Look it up first, then answer

The model doesn't know your documents, and its knowledge stops at its training cut-off. RAG fixes both: search your data for the relevant pieces, paste them into the context, and ask the model to answer from those pieces only.

Choosing

RAG, fine-tuning or long context

RAG for facts that change or need citing. Fine-tuning for style, format and narrow skills. Long context when everything fits and you query it rarely.

Failure modes

Where RAG breaks

Bad chunking splits answers in half. Questions use different words from the docs. Stale or conflicting chunks slip in. Most "the AI is wrong" bugs are retrieval bugs.

2026 practice

Agentic RAG

The model drives the search: decides when to look, rewrites queries, reads, searches again. Coding agents mostly use grep and file reads instead of vector databases.

Takeaway

RAG is search, paste, answer with citations. Measure retrieval separately from the answer; that is where problems hide.

12Intermediate

Tools & function calling

The model asks. Your code acts.

A model can only output text. Tool calling is a contract: you describe tools in the request, the model replies with a structured request to use one, your code runs it, and you send the result back. The model never touches the outside world directly.

A tool definition

{
  "name": "get_weather",
  "description": "Current weather for a city. Use when the
     user asks about weather, rain, or what to wear.",
  "input_schema": {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": { "city": { "type": "string" } },
    "required": ["city"]
  }
}

The description is a prompt. The model decides whether to call a tool by reading its name and description. Vague descriptions cause wrong or missing calls.

Fewer, sharper tools. Ten overlapping tools confuse models. Design them like a good API for a new colleague: clear names, clear errors, useful outputs.

Errors are information. Return "City not found, try the English spelling", not a stack trace. The model will read it and fix its call.

Structured outputs use the same machinery to force replies into a JSON schema your code can trust.

Takeaway

Tool calling turns a text generator into something that can act. Put it in a loop and you have an agent.

Part III · Chapters 13–23

Agents

An agent is a model using tools in a loop, choosing its own next step, until the job is done. Simple to say. The engineering is in everything around the loop.

13Advanced

The agent loop

Think, act, observe, repeat

A chatbot answers once. An agent keeps going: it gathers context, decides what to do, calls a tool, reads the result, and loops until the goal is met or a limit is hit. The pattern is often called ReAct, for reason and act.

Chatbot

One question, one answer

No tools, no loop. Good for drafting and Q&A.

Workflow

You write the steps

Code decides the path; the model fills steps in. Predictable, testable, cheap.

Agent

The model writes the steps

It picks tools and order at runtime. Flexible for open-ended work, harder to predict.

Stopping

Goal, limit or human

It stops when it answers without a tool call, hits a turn, token or dollar limit, or a person interrupts. Every production agent needs hard limits.

Takeaway

An agent is a model, tools, a loop and a stop condition. Its quality depends on how well it can check its own work.

14Advanced

The harness

The model is the engine. The harness is the car.

A harness is all the software wrapped around a model that turns it into a working agent: the loop, tools, instructions, context handling, memory, permissions and interface. Claude Code, Codex, Cursor and OpenClaw are harnesses. Same model, different harness, very different results.

A whole harness, minus the polish

messages = [{"role": "user", "content": task}]

while turns < MAX_TURNS:                # the loop
    reply = llm(system=SYSTEM_PROMPT,    # instructions
                tools=TOOLS,              # tool registry
                messages=messages)
    messages.append(reply)
    calls = [b for b in reply.content if b.type == "tool_use"]
    if not calls:
        break                             # model says it's done
    results = []
    for call in calls:
        if not permitted(call):           # permissions
            out = "Denied by policy"
        else:
            out = run_in_sandbox(call)   # sandbox
        results.append(tool_result(call.id, out))
    messages.append({"role": "user", "content": results})
    if tokens(messages) > LIMIT:
        messages = compact(messages)      # context management
    turns += 1

That really is the core. About twenty lines. Production harnesses are this loop plus thousands of lines of careful engineering behind each comment.

Harness design decides behaviour. Which tools exist, how outputs are trimmed, when to compact, what needs approval. Teams report large quality gains from harness changes alone, with the model unchanged.

Build or borrow. Use Claude Code, Codex or Cursor for coding. Use an SDK such as the Claude Agent SDK, OpenAI Agents SDK or Google ADK to build your own. Write the loop yourself once, to understand it (chapter 23).

Customise through files. Most harnesses read an instructions file (CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md), load skills, run hooks and connect MCP servers, so you shape the agent without touching its code.

Takeaway

When an agent disappoints, check the harness before blaming the model. What did it see, which tools did it have, what was it allowed to do?

15Advanced

Context engineering

The right tokens at the right time

Agents run for dozens or hundreds of turns. Each turn adds tool output, and every call re-reads everything. Left alone, the window fills with junk, quality drops and cost explodes. Context engineering is curating what the model sees at every step.

Write

Save it outside the window

Notes, to-do lists, progress files. The agent writes state to disk and re-reads only what it needs. This is how agents work for hours.

Select

Pull in just in time

Don't preload everything. Give the agent search tools and let it fetch the file it needs, when it needs it.

Compress

Summarise and trim

Compaction turns 150K tokens of history into a 5K summary. Truncate huge tool outputs. Keep decisions, drop noise.

Isolate

Split across windows

Subagents do messy exploration in their own context and hand back a clean answer (chapter 19).

Money

Prompt caching

Providers cache the start of your prompt (system prompt, tools, early history), so repeated calls re-read it at roughly a tenth of the normal input price, and faster. Put stable content first and changing content last, or the cache breaks.

Takeaway

The context window is a scarce, shared budget. Every token you add competes for attention and is paid for again on every future turn.

16Advanced

Memory

Memory is something the harness builds

The model forgets everything the moment a call ends. Memory is the harness deciding what to write down, where to keep it, and when to bring it back into context. Done well, the agent feels like it knows you. Done badly, it confidently recalls stale facts.

Storage

Where it lives

Plain files (CLAUDE.md, MEMORY.md) are simple and human-editable. Vector stores search by meaning. Databases and graphs hold structured facts and relationships.

Hard parts

Save, recall, forget

What is durable enough to save? What is relevant enough to recall? When is it stale? People move, prices change, code gets refactored.

Privacy

It is personal data

Let users see, edit and delete memory. Never let one user's memories leak into another's context.

Takeaway

Working memory is the context window. Long-term memory is files or databases the harness reads and writes. The model itself never remembers.

17Advanced

MCP, skills & A2A

One plug for every tool

The Model Context Protocol, released by Anthropic in November 2024 and now stewarded by the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, is a standard way to connect AI apps to tools and data. Build a GitHub connector once as an MCP server and every MCP-aware app can use it.

How it's built

Host: the AI app (Claude, ChatGPT, Cursor, VS Code, your agent). Client: the connector inside it. Server: a small program exposing a system such as GitHub, Postgres, Slack or an internal API.

Servers offer tools (actions), resources (data to read) and prompts (templates). They speak JSON-RPC over stdio for local processes or streamable HTTP for remote services, usually with OAuth.

Caution: an MCP server runs with real permissions, and its tool descriptions go straight into your model's context. Install only servers you trust.

// host → server: what can you do?
{"jsonrpc":"2.0","id":1,"method":"tools/list"}

// server → host
{"result":{"tools":[{"name":"create_issue",
  "description":"Open a GitHub issue",
  "inputSchema":{…}}]}}

// later, when the model decides to use it
{"method":"tools/call","params":{
  "name":"create_issue",
  "arguments":{"title":"Checkout tax bug"}}}
Skills

Know-how in a folder

A SKILL.md with instructions, plus optional scripts and templates. Only its name and one-line description sit in context; the rest loads when a task needs it. Introduced by Anthropic in October 2025, now an open format.

AGENTS.md · CLAUDE.md

A README for agents

A plain file in your repo explaining how to build, test and follow your conventions. Loaded at the start of every session.

A2A

Agents talking to agents

Google's Agent2Agent protocol (2025) lets agents from different vendors find each other through "agent cards" and delegate tasks. MCP connects agents to tools; A2A connects agents to agents.

Takeaway

MCP for tools and data, skills for know-how, AGENTS.md for house rules, A2A for coworkers. Together they plug agents into the rest of your stack.

18Advanced

Workflows vs agents

Autonomy is a dial, not a switch

The best teams start with the simplest thing that works and add autonomy only when the task demands it. Turn the dial, then study the six patterns behind most real systems.

Takeaway

One good call, then a workflow, then an agent, then several agents. Climb only as far as the task forces you to.

19Advanced

Multi-agent systems

Many agents, one goal

One agent has one context window and works one step at a time. A multi-agent system splits a big job across several agents working in parallel, each with a clean window, coordinated by an orchestrator. Faster and broader, but it burns far more tokens.

Helps

Wide, separable work

Research across many sources, reviewing many files, trying several approaches at once. Each worker's clean context is often worth more than the speed.

Hurts

Tightly coupled work

One intricate code change, or anything where agents must agree on shared details. They step on each other and drift apart.

Coordination

How agents talk

Through the orchestrator's messages, shared files or task boards, and across vendors through A2A. Keep hand-offs short and structured.

Cost

Budget for it

Anthropic reported its multi-agent research system used about 15× the tokens of a normal chat. Worth it for high-value questions, not for everyday ones.

Takeaway

Parallel agents trade tokens for time and focus. Use them when the work splits cleanly and the answer is worth the bill.

20Advanced

Coding & computer-use agents

Agents that write code and drive computers

Coding agents became everyday tools first because code offers something rare: a fast, objective check. Tests pass or fail. Computer-use agents stretch the same loop to any screen: look, think, click, look again.

A day with a coding agent

Step 1

Explore and plan

Ask it to read the relevant code and propose a plan before touching anything. Review the plan; it is cheaper to fix than the code.

Step 2

Give it house rules

A CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md with build commands, test commands, conventions and "never do" rules.

Step 3

Small steps, with checks

Implement in pieces, running tests, type checks and linters after each. Hooks can enforce this automatically.

Step 4

Verify like a user

For UI work, have it run the app and look at screenshots. For APIs, call the endpoints.

Step 5

Review the diff

Read what changed as you would a colleague's pull request. You own what ships.

Step 6

Parallelise

Run several agents on separate git worktrees or in the cloud, each on its own task, then review their pull requests.

Takeaway

Agents thrive where results can be checked. Give them a way to verify their own work and they will outperform any number of clever prompts.

21Advanced

The toolbox & frameworks

The 2026 stack, layer by layer

Every buzzword you hear sits somewhere in this stack. Choose a layer to see who plays there. Most products need one model provider, one SDK or framework, one place for data, and one way to watch what happens.

Starting out

Use the raw API first

Call the provider's SDK directly and add tool calling. You'll learn more, faster, than with any framework.

Agents in code

An SDK or a graph

Claude Agent SDK or OpenAI Agents SDK for batteries-included loops. LangGraph for explicit state machines. Pydantic AI or Mastra for lean, typed code.

Business automation

A visual builder

n8n, Zapier or Make: workflows with AI steps and hundreds of integrations, no deep coding needed.

Takeaway

Frameworks change every year. The concepts under them, loop, tools, context, memory and evals, don't. Learn those, then pick what your team can maintain.

22Advanced

n8n & OpenClaw

The workflow canvas and the personal agent

n8n puts AI inside visual, auditable business workflows. OpenClaw puts an always-on agent on your own computer that you message like a friend. They sit at opposite ends of the autonomy dial.

What n8n is. An open, self-hostable workflow automation tool, a cousin of Zapier and Make, with hundreds of integrations. Its AI Agent node lets a model choose among tools you attach, with a chat model and memory plugged in as sub-nodes. It can call MCP servers or expose a workflow as one.

Why teams like it. Every step is visible and every run is logged. Non-engineers can follow it, and you can wrap deterministic checks and approvals around the AI step: guardrails you can see.

Why it spread

An agent where you already talk

Created by Peter Steinberger (first called Clawdbot, then Moltbot), it showed what a proactive, always-on personal agent feels like: it books, reminds, researches, files and codes while you are away.

The lesson

Power and risk share a source

It reads untrusted messages, can reach private files, and can send messages and run commands: the lethal trifecta (chapter 24). Researchers found exposed instances and malicious community skills soon after launch. Run it sandboxed, with least privilege.

Takeaway

In n8n, people design the path and AI fills in steps. In OpenClaw, AI designs the path and people set the boundaries. Most businesses need the first before the second.

Part IV · Chapters 24–27

Production

A demo works once. A product works ten thousand times a day, safely, at a known cost, and you can prove it.

24Pro

Guardrails & policies

Stack imperfect layers until almost nothing gets through

No single defence stops every bad input or action. Safety engineers call this the Swiss-cheese model: each layer has holes, but the holes rarely line up. Toggle the layers and watch the leak rate.

The lethal trifecta

Coined by Simon Willison: an agent with access to private data that reads untrusted content (web pages, emails, issues, documents) and can communicate externally can be tricked into leaking your data. Nobody has a complete fix for prompt injection yet.

The practical rule: remove at least one of the three, or put a human or a hard code check on the outbound path.

Policies: decide before the incident

Action riskExamplesPolicy
Read-only, internalSearch docs, read code, summarise ticketsAuto-approve and log
Reversible writesDraft an email, edit a file on a branch, open a ticketAuto-approve inside a sandbox; review in batches
External or costlySend email, post publicly, spend money, call paid APIsHuman approval per action, with a spending cap
Irreversible or regulatedDelete data, move funds, change permissions, medical or legal adviceForbidden for agents, or two-person approval
Data policy

What goes where

Which data may reach which provider? Retention agreements, regional hosting, PII redaction before prompts.

Acceptable use

What it refuses

What your product declines and how it says so. Written down, tested with evals, reviewed each quarter.

Audit

Log everything

Every prompt, tool call and approval. When something goes wrong you need the full trace, not a guess.

Takeaway

Treat the model as a talented intern who can be sweet-talked by strangers. Let it do a lot, but put the hard limits in code, not only in the prompt.

25Pro

Evals & observability

Evals are unit tests for behaviour

"It looked good when I tried it" is not a measurement. Evals are repeatable test sets that score your AI system; traces show exactly what happened on each run. Together they let you change prompts, models or tools without breaking things.

The eval flywheel

Start with 20 real examples, not 2,000 synthetic ones. Take them from real usage, and especially from failures.

Three kinds of grader. Code checks (did the JSON parse, did the tests pass). A model as judge, scoring against a rubric. Humans, for the subtle cases and to calibrate the judge.

Measure the agent, not only the answer: task success, right tool with right arguments, turns, tokens, dollars, latency and safety violations.

Tools: LangSmith, Langfuse, Braintrust, Arize Phoenix and OpenTelemetry tracing each cover some mix of traces, datasets and graders.

Takeaway

No evals, no product. Every production bug becomes a test case, so it can never quietly return.

26Pro

Cost, speed & model choice

Tokens × turns × traffic = your bill

AI costs scale with usage. A demo costs cents. The same agent serving ten thousand users, looping fifteen times per task over a growing context, can cost more than the engineers who built it. Work the numbers before you launch.

Model tiers

Smallest model that passes your evals

Frontier (Opus, GPT, Gemini Pro class) for hard reasoning and long agent runs. Mid-tier (Sonnet class) as the workhorse. Small and fast (Haiku, Flash, mini class) for routing, classification and extraction. Open weights for privacy and fixed cost at volume.

Levers, easiest first

Where savings come from

Prompt caching. Trim context and tool outputs. Lower the thinking effort where quality holds. Route easy requests to smaller models. Batch APIs for non-urgent work, often around half price. Fewer turns through better tools.

Takeaway

Latency and cost both grow with tokens. The cheapest token is the one you never send.

27Pro

Reference architecture

What a production agent system actually looks like

Behind every serious AI product is ordinary, sturdy software: gateways, queues, databases, sandboxes and dashboards arranged around the model. Click any block, or trace one request from end to end.

State

Stateless workers, durable state

Agent runs live in a queue and a database, not in memory, so a crash resumes instead of restarting a two-hour task.

Models

A model gateway

One internal endpoint for every provider: routing, fallbacks when one is down, key management, per-team budgets and caching.

Isolation

Sandbox per task

Code execution and browsing run in short-lived containers with no secrets and limited network.

Visibility

Everything traced

Business & you

Technology is the easier half. The harder half is choosing where to apply it, building something defensible, and learning in the right order.

28Pro

AI in your business

Put AI where the work is, not where the hype is

The winning move in most companies is not a moonshot. It is finding the boring, high-volume, text-heavy work that eats hours every week, and automating it step by step, measuring at each step.

Step 1

Map the work

List recurring tasks by team: who does it, how often, how long, what inputs, what "done" looks like.

Step 2

Score it

Hours saved and repetitiveness, against the cost of a mistake and whether the data is reachable.

Step 3

Pick the level

Assist (AI drafts, a person sends), automate (workflow with checks), or agent (AI decides, a person audits).

Step 4

Pilot narrowly

One team, one task, two to four weeks, with an eval set and a baseline. Grow it or kill it on the numbers.

Step 5

Redesign the process

The big gains come from rebuilding the workflow around AI, not bolting AI onto the old one.

Step 6

Scale and govern

A shared platform, the policies from chapter 24, cost dashboards, and training so every team can build.

Takeaway

Start with a painful, frequent, measurable task. Ship something small that saves real hours. Credibility compounds faster than ambition.

29Pro

The billion-dollar question

How AI companies actually get big

Nobody can promise a billion dollars. But the fastest-growing AI companies share patterns, and the ones that died share others. Several AI-native startups passed $100M in annual revenue with small teams. Leverage is real, and so is the risk of building something the next model makes free.

Thin wrapper

Dies when the model improves

A good prompt on someone else's API, with no proprietary data, no workflow lock-in, and nothing hard to copy. The next model release or platform feature absorbs it.

Compounding product

Improves when the model improves

Owns the workflow, the data, the integrations and the trust. Ask: "If the model got ten times smarter tomorrow, would we win more, or lose?"

Business models that work in 2026

Pricing

Sell the outcome

Charge per resolved ticket, qualified lead or reviewed contract. Selling work done captures far more value than selling seats.

Focus

Vertical agents

Deep in one industry (legal, dental, logistics, insurance) with its data formats, regulations and integrations. Horizontal chat is crowded; verticals are not.

Services

AI-enabled services

An agency, accounting firm or support centre where agents do most of the work. Service margins move toward software margins.

Infrastructure

Picks and shovels

Evals, observability, security, data pipelines, MCP servers, compute. Everyone building agents needs them.

Internal

Leverage as a moat

A 50-person company operating like a 200-person one is a competitive advantage in itself.

Go-to-market

Distribution first

If you already own an audience or customer relationships, AI features can be the fastest path to revenue.

The traps

Trap

Demo is not product

Reaching 80% takes a weekend. The last 20%, edge cases, reliability and security, takes the year.

Trap

Unit economics

Agent costs scale with usage. Price so your heaviest users are still profitable.

Trap

Trust debt

One public mistake, a leaked record or a rogue email, can cost more than a year of growth.

Takeaway

Build what gets better as models get better. Own the workflow and the data, charge for outcomes, and earn trust faster than you spend it.

30Pro

The AI-native company

Smaller teams, longer reach

When agents handle much of the execution, the shape of a company changes: fewer layers of coordination, small senior teams each directing many agents, and new roles for designing, supervising and evaluating agent work.

New roles

Who you hire

Agent operations, eval engineers, AI product managers, forward-deployed engineers who embed agents with customers.

Management

What managers do

Set goals, design processes, review outcomes, handle exceptions. Less status-chasing, more judgement.

Metrics

What you measure

Revenue per employee, cycle time, share of work agent-assisted, cost per outcome, error and escalation rates.

Change

How it spreads

Train everyone, share wins publicly, write the policies early, and let teams build their own agents on a shared platform.

What to watch next

Autonomy

Longer tasks

METR measured the length of tasks AI can complete doubling roughly every seven months through 2025. Plan for agents that run for days.

Economics

Cheaper intelligence

The price of a given level of capability keeps falling fast. Tasks that are too expensive today will be cheap within a year.

Commerce

Agents as customers

Agents that compare, negotiate and buy on someone's behalf. Your product may soon need an interface for them too.

Rules

Regulation

The EU AI Act's obligations phase in from 2025 to 2027, and other regions are following. Governance is becoming a product feature.

Takeaway

The question stops being "how many people do we need?" and becomes "how much good judgement can we apply, and how many agents can each person direct well?"

What to learn, in order

Your roadmap from beginner to pro

A four-week starter plan

Week 1

Use it daily, then call the API

Use an assistant for real work every day. Then write a short script that calls a model and returns JSON.

Week 2

Add tools and data

Give your script two tools and a small RAG over your own notes. Break it on purpose and read the logs.

Week 3

Write the loop

Follow chapter 23. Add a turn limit, a permission check and a memory file. Wrap one tool as an MCP server.

Week 4

Make it real

Pick one task from your job. Write 20 eval cases. Ship to one real user. Track cost and success rate.

Ten things you must understand

  1. The model is stateless.Every call starts from zero. Context is all it knows.
  2. It predicts plausible, not true.Ground it with sources and tools, then verify.
  3. Agent = model + tools + loop.The harness matters as much as the model.
  4. Write for a smart new hire.Clear context and reasons beat clever tricks.
  5. Simplest thing first.Call, workflow, agent, multi-agent. Climb only when needed.
  6. No evals, no product.Measure before and after every change.
  7. Break the lethal trifecta.Untrusted input, private data and outbound action don't mix.
  8. Cost = tokens × turns × traffic.Cache, trim, route.
  9. Build for the next model.Don't over-engineer around today's weaknesses.
  10. People own the outcome.Design where humans review, approve and take responsibility.
